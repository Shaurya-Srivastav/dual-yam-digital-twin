Two arms. One dry dispenser.
No cameras.

A MuJoCo digital twin of a two-arm (I2RT YAM) water-cup station. One arm brings a paper or plastic cup under the spout, the other lifts a 360° lever for a 7-second pour, and the cup is served into a coaster. Every skill works from what real motors report: joint position, velocity, torque and gripper width.

The papers this is built on

The skills are blind on purpose. These are the papers that say how to localise and manipulate from touch and joint torque alone, and what this twin actually took from each one. Full notes are in docs/RESEARCH.md.

Finding a cup by touch

  • Memory Unscented Particle Filter for 6-DOF Tactile Localization — Vezzani, Pattacini, Battistelli, Chisci, Natale, IEEE Transactions on Robotics, 2017. arXiv:1607.02757. A particle belief over object pose, updated from contact.
  • An Empirical Evaluation of Various Information Gain Criteria for Active Tactile Action Selection for Pose Estimation — Murali, Dahiya, Kaboli, 2022. HTML. Pick the next touch by how much it should shrink the belief. They report under 1 cm after 15 touches.
  • Touch-Based Object Localisation with Spatially-Aware Belief Entropy Estimation — Brudermüller, Jankowski, Toussaint, Hawes, ICRA 2026. Touch-only global localisation, then a grasp.

What runs here: every peck rules out cup centres the arm and cup models cannot explain (the hard-likelihood, set-membership version of that belief). The next peck is the one that splits the centres still in play about in half. It is not a particle filter, and it does not compute expected entropy over a full pose distribution.

Finding a handle without moving it

  • Underactuated Robotic Hand with Grasp State Estimation Using Tendon-Based Proprioception — Lee, Park, Cho, 2025. HTML. Pause the first finger that touches, so the object is not pushed before the grasp is set.
  • Revisiting Proprioceptive Sensing for Articulated Object Manipulation — Lips and wyffels, ICRA 2023 workshop. Estimate a joint from the gripper pose while in contact. Slip between gripper and handle is the failure they name.
  • Contact State Estimation using Machine Learning — Jamali, Kormushev, Caldwell, OCEANS 2013. DOI. Classify no contact, on-axis, and off-centre contact on a T-bar valve from force and torque.

What runs here: the fingertip stops at about 0.9 N, under the roughly 1.5 N it takes to turn the swivel, then the handle yaw is solved from the known hand and handle shapes. The threshold is still below a learned contact classifier.

Turning a cup over with one arm

  • In-Hand Gravitational Pivoting Using Tactile Sensing — Toskov, Newbury, Mukadam, Kulić, Cosgun, CoRL 2022. HTML. Let a held object rotate in a parallel gripper under gravity, tracked from touch.

What runs here is not that method. The wrist is turned to the flip attitude before the fingers close, the cup is lifted clear of the stack, and one Cartesian roll turns it upright. The attitude came from an inverse-kinematics search, not from the paper. Gravitational pivoting is the alternative that was not used: it needs slip inside the fingers, and this grasp is meant to hold.

Dance mode

The live dashboard plays the audio file and reports its clock. The server tracks tempo, beats, downbeats and loudness from that audio and poses both arms from phase-locked moves: sway, pump, wave, circle, point and clap, sized by the song's energy. The clips here carry no audio track: owning a copy of a song does not give the right to republish it, so the recordings are not in the public videos, the repo or this site. To hear them, open a clip and press Play my own copy: pick the song file on your computer and the page plays it in sync with the arms, without uploading it. The dots are the tracked beat. The local dashboard does the same from files on that machine when you press Start dance.

How it works

1 · Plant1 kHz MuJoCo, MIT-style law τ = kp(q*−q) + kd(q̇*−q̇) + gravity. Friction, mass, gains, base pose, sensor noise and delay are randomized per trial; power-up calibration fits the gravity feed-forward from position sag.
2 · SenseContact is the torque residual after the controller's own gravity and friction model, mapped to a Cartesian force with −pinv(Jᵀ). Guards use force steps, never a true contact flag.
3 · PlanStrict mink IK, min-jerk synchronized trajectories, Cartesian lines for the last centimetres, collision checks against the bench, the dispenser and the other arm.
4 · SkillsGenerators, one yield per 2 ms tick: side grasp, spout insertion, lever routine, coaster place, pecks, handle search, one-arm flip. Failures are loud.
5 · LevelsA dependency graph over two arm lanes. The tap arm moves to the lever while the cup is still being carried.
6 · RefereeOnly the referee reads true poses: upright, centred, 7.0 s pour with the cup under the outlet, dispenser still, no collisions, arms at rest.

Finding the lever without moving it

From contact-pause grasping and proprioceptive articulated-object estimation: set the threshold above noise but below breakaway. Jamali, Kormushev and Caldwell classify T-bar contact the same way, from force rather than vision.

  • A fingertip circles the valve and stops at about 0.9 N; the swivel needs about 1.5 N to turn.
  • The handle yaw is solved from the known hand and handle shapes (oriented-box intersection), not a fixed offset.
  • The finger pushes it to about 45°, backs off and touches again to confirm; if it is lost it searches again.
  • The swivel turns a full 360°; tested from −170° to 175°.

Finding the cup without cameras

Vezzani et al. keep a belief and update it from contact. Murali, Dahiya and Kaboli and Brudermüller et al. choose the next touch by information gain.

  • Pecks from above on a coarse grid until something stands up. The approach is nearly vertical, and a miss stops at 8 cm, so the wrist housing never drops onto the cup.
  • Every peck rules out centres the arm and cup models cannot explain. The next peck splits the centres still in play about in half.
  • The fit is set-membership. It is not the particle filter in the 2017 paper, and it does not estimate expected entropy.

The one-arm flip

The wrist is turned the other way, about −65° off the arm's line and 8° down, before the fingers touch the cup. Gravitational pivoting is the paper alternative; this roll does not use it.

  • The cup is held by its base band. Because the hand is already in the flip attitude, the lift itself rolls the cup over.
  • The roll is a Cartesian path: rise clear of the stack, slide 9 cm off it, roll 170° (the last 10° is past the wrist), then lower. One arm only.
  • The wrist housing cannot follow the base the last few centimetres, so the cup is released a few centimetres up and drops onto its base.

Side grasp that does not push the cup

  • Slides in with open jaws and complies sideways to contact force so the funnel pads centre the hand.
  • On closing, the stall width gives the chord 2·√(r²−s²): how far off the cup's axis the hand is, so depth is servoed from touch alone.
  • Force caps per grip; no weld, no snap.

What is still open, and why

  • Cup more than 15 mm from the coaster centre (the most common failure in Levels 2 and 3, 13 of 48 trials). The coaster is placed up to 8 mm off its nominal spot in randomized trials and the skills are not told where. The dish has about 2 cm of slack around the cup base, which the real replay needs, so it does not centre the cup. A four-sided touch of the dish wall with the held cup was tried and made it worse; it is not in this build.
  • Level 4: dispenser pushed more than 2 mm (7 of 24) while the fingertip parks the handle, and the lever lift's first IK step jumps 0.13 rad on some draws (3 of 24). Making the taped dispenser stiffer fixed the creep but broke the lever close timing in Level 1, so it was taken out.
  • Level 3: no collision-free pin approach for some stack heights (5 of 24).
  • Level 5: a grid peck that lands inside the cup's open mouth reads as "no cup here", so the scan can miss the cup or fit it in the wrong place and the grasp closes on nothing. That is 8 of 24 failures. The other Level 5 failures are the two arms touching (3), the hand brushing the parked stack (3) and the grasp closing on nothing (3). An offset second pass and model-based pruning were tried and lowered the rate (8 %); they are not in this build.
  • Grasp failures in every level (1–2 of 24): the jaws stall on a 61–63 mm chord or close on nothing after nine attempts.
  • Not separate implementations: the arms are MuJoCo position actuators with the MIT law packed into the command, not torque actuators; there are no bimanual zone locks beyond the planner's collision checks; there is no GripperForceLimiter class (force caps are inside the grip routine). The Level 5 fit is set-membership with a split-the-candidates rule, not a particle filter or expected entropy.
  • Dimensions not measured on the real station: the setup photos are not in the tree and were not used to override the forward-kinematics millimetres. Tap-arm base, dish radius and spigot geometry are inferred.
  • No hardware run. SimYam implements the i2rt Robot protocol and the same driver can target get_yam_robot, but it is untested on the arms.

IK equivalence

For all 12 taught Level 1 poses, strict Cartesian IK from unrelated seeds reproduces the real hand pose: ≤ 0.4 mm and 0.01°, so the skills use Cartesian goals, not cloned joint angles.

Run the live dashboard

The interactive twin (30–50 fps view with a view cube, step timeline with scrub-back, telemetry charts, belief-vs-truth overlay, Monte Carlo panel and Dance Mode) needs a Python process running MuJoCo, which a static host cannot run.

git clone https://github.com/Shaurya-Srivastav/dual-yam-digital-twin
cd dual-yam-digital-twin
git clone https://github.com/i2rt-robotics/i2rt third_party/i2rt     # arm and gripper models
pip install -r requirements.txt                                     # Python 3.11; ffmpeg for Dance Mode
./run_sim.sh                                                        # http://127.0.0.1:8090
python -m sim_station.selftest                                      # all levels + real-data replay
